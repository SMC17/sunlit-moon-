The [Lakefront Pavilion](/developments/lakefront-pavilion-restoration) reopened on an August Saturday that behaved like a postcard and then, because this is a Great Lake, did not.

Cedar is back on the roof. Limestone that had spalled in the freeze-thaw of a decade has been pieced. The lake-facing openings — boarded, then meshed, then argued over — are daylight again. Status: **Complete**. In a city that lives in the gerund of construction, completion is a kind of news.

The pavilion was never grand. That is the point. A 1930s shelter at Clark Street Beach, it was a roof for a picnic and a windbreak for a March that had not read the calendar. Restoration returned it to that modest job. Architects talk about “original openings.” Beach regulars talk about the bench that got the afternoon.

## A public sequence

[Evanston’s lakefront](/places/evanston-lakefront) is not a single park. It is a sequence: Calvary’s edge, the beaches, the pavilion, the [Grosse Point Lighthouse](/places/grosse-point-lighthouse), and then the private-public landscape of the [Northwestern campus](/places/northwestern-campus) fill. You can walk most of it. You cannot walk all of it without negotiating a university that is also a landscape.

The lighthouse is the photogenic argument — 1873 brick, a beam that still means something after GPS, grounds that remind visitors that navigation was once a civic art. The pavilion is the ordinary one. Ordinary public buildings are the ones a city actually uses.

## What restoration returns

There is a temptation to treat a finished restoration as nostalgia. The better reading is maintenance as policy. A city that can finish a small historic roof is a city that might, on a later Tuesday, finish a plaza.

From the pavilion’s lake openings you cannot see Fountain Square. You can see the argument that connects them: Evanston is a place that faces water and still has to decide what its rooms are for — the grand ones and the ones with a cedar roof and a bench that gets the afternoon.
